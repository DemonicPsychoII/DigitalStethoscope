<h2>Background</h2>

<p>Heart sound segmentation refers to the detection of the exact positions of the first (S<sub>1</sub>) and second (S<sub>2</sub>) heart sounds in a heart sound recording or phonocardiogram (PCG).
These sounds originate at the beginning of mechanical systole and diastole respectively [1], with S<sub>1</sub> occuring immediately after the R-peak (ventricular depolarisation) of the electrocardiogram (ECG), while S<sub>2</sub> occurs at approximately at the end-T-wave of the ECG (the end of ventricular depolarisation) [2] (See Figure 1).
</p>

<p>Heart sound segmentation is an essential step in the automatic analysis of heart sound recordings, as it allows the analysis of the periods between these sounds for the presence of clicks and murmurs; sounds often indicative of pathology [3]. Segmentation becomes a difficult task when  PCG recordings are corrupted by in-band noise. Common noise sources include endogenous or ambient speech, motion artefacts and physiological sounds such as intestinal and breathing sounds. Other physiological sounds of interest, such as murmurs, clicks, splitting of the heart sounds and additional S<sub>3</sub>
  and S<sub>4</sub> sounds, can also complicate the identification of the S<sub>1</sub> and S<sub>2</sub> sounds. For example, Figure 1 shows a PCG (alongside an ECG) with a loud mid-systolic click indicative of mitral valve prolapse, which could be confused for S<sub>1</sub> or S<sub>2</sub>.
</p> 
  
<div style="width: 600px; margin: 0 auto;"><img src="Figure1.png" width="600" alt="[Example of an ECG-labelled PCG]">

<p style="font-style: italic"><strong>Figure 1</strong>: Example of an ECG-labelled PCG, with the ECG, PCG and four states of the heart cycle (S<sub>1</sub>, systole, S<sub>2</sub> and diastole) shown. A mid-systolic click can be seen in each of the heart cycles, labelled as "m". The R-peak and end-T-wave are labelled as references for defining the approximate positions of S<sub>1</sub> and S<sub>2</sub> respectively.
</p>
</div>
  
<p>While threshold-based
segmentation methods have shown modest success, probabilistic models,
such as hidden Markov models (HMMs), have recently been shown to surpass
the capabilities of previous methods. Segmentation performance is
further improved when <i>a priori</i> information about the expected
duration of the states is incorporated into the model, such as in
a hidden semi-Markov model (HSMM) [4]. An excellent introduction to HMMs can be found in [5].</p>

<p>The code on this page extends the work of [4] by implementing such a HSMM for segmentation, but extended with the use of logistic regression for emission probability estimation which was found to significantly improve segmentation accuracy. In addition, we implement a modified Viterbi algorithm for decoding the most-likely sequence of states. This modified Viterbi algorithm overcomes the limitation of the standard duration-dependant Viterbi algorithm where the start and end of detected states have to coincide with the start and end of the PCG recording. More details can be found in the paper referenced above.</p>




<h2>Explanation of the code</h2>

<p>
The code at the bottom of this page can be used to both train and execute the HSMM-based segmentation of PCGs. The step-by-step operations of the code are explained below.</p>

<h3>Training the HSMM</h3>
<p>
The HSMM training is performed within the <code>trainSpringerSegmentationAlgorithm.m</code> file. Within this file, the basic steps are:</p>
<ol>
    <li>Extract the features from the training set of recordings (<code>getSpringerPCGFeatures.m</code>). These include the:
    <ul>
        <li>Homomorphic envelope</li>
        <li>Hilbert envelope</li>
        <li>Power spectral density envelope</li>
        <li>Discrete wavelet transform envelope</li>
    </ul></li>
    <li>Label the PCGs based on the supplied R-peak and end-T-wave locations (<code>labelPCGStates.m</code>).</li>
    <li>Train the three parameter matrices for the HSMM (<code>trainBandPiMatricesSpringer.m</code>).</li>
</ol>



<h3>Running the trained HSMM</h3>
<p>
The three trained parameter matrices from above are used to segment a PCG in <code>runSpringerSegmentationAlgorithm.m</code>. The basic operations of the segmentation are:
</p>

<ol>
    <li>Extract the features from a test set recording (<code>getSpringerPCGFeatures.m</code>).</li>
    <li>Estimate the heart rate based on the autocorrelation (<code>getHeartRateSchmidt.m</code>).</li>
    <li>Use the extended duration-dependant Viterbi algorithm to derive the most likely sequence of states from the PCG (<code>lviterbiDecodePCG_Springer.m</code>).
<!--     <ul type "a"> -->
    <ul>
        <li>The duration distributions for the four states used in the Viterbi algorithm are computed in <code>get_duration_distributions.m</code>.
        </li>
    </ul>
    </li>
</ol>

<p>
An example of the output of <code>runSpringerSegmentationAlgorithm.m</code> is shown in blue at the top of Figure 2.
</p>


<p>
Important options for the code are stored in <code>default_Springer_HSMM_options.m</code>. These include:
</p>

<ul>
    <li>The original sampling frequency of the PCG signals.</li>
    <li>The sampling frequency to which to downsample the segmentation features.</li>
    <li>Whether <code>.mex</code> code should be used for the Viterbi algorithm.</li>
    <li>Whether wavelet features should be used (as this toolbox may be missing from your Matlab installation).</li>
</ul>

<p><b>If you set <code>springer_options.use_mex = true;</code> in the options file, please be sure to create the MEX executable from the source file by running: <code>mex viterbi_Springer.c</code> before running the code.</b>
</p>


<div style="width: 600px; margin: 0 auto;"><img src="Figure2.png" width="600" alt="[Example of a segmented PCG]">
<p style="font-style: italic"><strong>Figure 2</strong>: Example of a segmented PCG using the code on this page. The four HSMM-detected states of the heart cycle (S<sub>1</sub>, systole, S<sub>2</sub> and diastole) are shown at the top of the plot in blue - this is the output of the <code>runSpringerSegmentationAlgorithm.m</code> file. The red dotted line shows the labelled states, derived from the R-peak and end-T-wave positions in the <code>labelPCGStates.m</code> file. These labelled states are only used for training the algorithm. The ECG is shown in red at the bottom of the plot, with the positions of the R-peaks (*) and the end-T-waves (o) indicated. Only the PCG is used to derive the detected states in blue.</p>
</div>


<h2>Quick start guide and example usage</h2>
<p>
An example of how the model is first trained on a small example set of data, and then used to segment a test recording is given in <code>run_Example_Springer_Script.m</code>. This should run out-of-the-box (provided you have set the <code>default_Springer_HSMM_options.m</code> correctly). <b>If you set <code>springer_options.use_mex = true;</code> in the options file, please be sure to create the MEX executable from the source file by running: <code>mex viterbi_Springer.c</code> before running the code.</b></p>

<p>
This code loads a few records from the example data. Alongside each recording are the R-peak and end-T-wave annotations which are used to label the locations of the heart sounds in the training set.</p>

<p>
The HSMM is trained on five of the six example recordings, and applied to the sixth. Running this script should produce a plot similar to that shown in Figure 3.
</p>

<div style="width: 600px; margin: 0 auto;">
<img src="Figure3.png" width="400"  alt="[Output of run_Example_Springer_Script.m]" style="margin-left: 8%;">
<p style="font-style: italic"><strong>Figure 3</strong>: The output when running <code>run_Example_Springer_Script.m</code>. This shows the PCG signal in black, with the HSMM derived states shown in red.</p>
</div>
       

<p>
The code in <code>run_Example_Springer_Script.m</code> will give you a clear example of how to apply these method to new datasets. Tested April 4 2016. 
</p>



<h2>Example Data</h2>
<p>
The <code>example_data.mat</code> file contains the full dataset from the paper mentioned at the top of this page. Included in this .mat file is a struct with the audio files, annotations, binary diagnosis (1 = presence of pathology) and the "patient number" for each record.
</p>

<p>
The <code>example_data.example_audio_data</code> is sampled at 1000 Hz, and contains recordings from various locations on the chest. While there are 135 patients, there are 792 recordings. This is due to multiple recordings per patient, as well as some recordings being split at positions of inconsistent annotations.
</p>

<p>
The <code>example_data.example_annotations</code> are the positions of the R-peak (first column of the cell array) and the end-T-wave positions (second column of the cell array), sampled at 50 Hz. As explained in the associated publication, these annotations are computed based on the agreement between five different automatic R-peak and end-T-wave detectors. They are not human-derived annotations.
</p>

<p>
The <code>example_data.binary_diagnosis</code> cell array shows which recordings were from subjects with no pathological heart damage as assessed by echocardiography (binary_diagnosis=0), and those with some sort of heart pathology (most commonly mitral valve prolapse - binary_diagnosis = 1).
</p>

<p>
The <code>example_data.patient_number</code> cell array indicates which subject each recording was captured from. Again, there are multiple recordings per patient due to multiple auscultation positions and splitting of recordings due to inconsistent annotations.
</p>
